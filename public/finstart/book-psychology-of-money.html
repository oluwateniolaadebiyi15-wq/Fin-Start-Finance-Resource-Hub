The Psychology of Money

by Morgan Housel

2020 256 pages Intermediate Investing

Overview

Morgan Housel's book is unlike almost any other finance title on the market. It doesn't tell you which stocks to buy, how to budget, or what percentage to save. Instead, it explores the strange ways people think about money — why smart, rational people make consistently irrational financial decisions, and what behavioural patterns actually separate the financially secure from everyone else. It's part psychology, part history, part gentle philosophy.

The book is structured as 19 short, self-contained stories or essays — each one illuminating a different aspect of how human behaviour shapes financial outcomes. You'll learn about the role of luck and risk, why long time horizons are the most powerful force in investing, and why "reasonable" beats "rational" when it comes to real-life money decisions. It's among the most readable finance books ever written.

Key Takeaways

1

Wealth is what you don't see. The true measure of financial success is unspent savings and invisible assets, not the car in the driveway.

2

Compounding is counterintuitive: Warren Buffett's wealth is almost entirely a function of time, not genius. He's been investing since age 10.

3

You are not your neighbour. People's financial decisions are shaped by their personal experiences with money growing up — rational for them, irrational to you.

4

"Reasonable > Rational": a financial strategy you can stick with through a bear market beats the mathematically optimal strategy you'll abandon in panic.

5

Saving is about having options and flexibility, not accumulating things. The highest dividend money pays is the ability to control your time.

"The ability to do what you want, when you want, with who you want, for as long as you want, is priceless. It is the highest dividend money pays."
— Morgan Housel
